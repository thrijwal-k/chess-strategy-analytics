# Middlegame ideas explained

## Pawn structure
The pawn structure is where the pawns stand. Pawns cannot move backwards, so the structure changes slowly and decides
the long-term plans. Many classic advantages and weaknesses are about pawns.

## Isolated pawn
An isolated pawn has no friendly pawns on the files next to it, so no pawn can ever protect it. It is often a long-term
weakness. In this project each extra isolated pawn costs about 0.2 percentage points of score.

## Isolated queen pawn (IQP)
An isolated queen pawn is an isolated pawn on the d-file. It gives its owner space and open lines for attacking, but it
can become a target in the endgame. In this project having one costs about 0.5 percentage points.

## Doubled pawns
Doubled pawns are two pawns of the same colour on the same file, usually after a capture. They cannot protect each
other and are often weak. Each extra doubled pawn costs about 0.6 percentage points here.

## Backward pawn
A backward pawn is one whose neighbours have advanced past it and which cannot safely move forward because the square
in front is controlled by an enemy pawn. It is hard to defend and the square in front of it can become an outpost for
the opponent.

## Passed pawn
A passed pawn has no enemy pawns in front of it on its own file or the files next to it, so nothing but pieces can
stop it from promoting. Passed pawns become more valuable as pieces are traded.

## Pawn islands
A pawn island is a group of pawns on neighbouring files. Fewer islands usually means a healthier structure that is
easier to defend. Each extra island costs about 0.8 percentage points in this project.

## Hanging pawns
Hanging pawns are two friendly pawns side by side on the c- and d-files with no friendly pawns on the files either
side. They control the centre but can be attacked.

## Bishop pair
Having both bishops while the opponent does not is called the bishop pair. Together the two bishops cover squares of
both colours, which is strong in open positions. In this project it is worth about 2.5 percentage points of score.

## Good and bad bishops
A bishop is called bad when many of its own pawns stand on squares of the same colour as the bishop, blocking it. A
good bishop moves freely in front of its pawns.

## Knight outpost
An outpost is a square in the opponent's half that is protected by your pawn and can never be attacked by an enemy
pawn. A knight on an outpost is very strong. It is the most valuable middlegame feature in this project, worth about
2.9 percentage points.

## Open files and the seventh rank
An open file has no pawns on it. Rooks are strongest on open files (no pawns at all) or half-open files (no pawns of
their own side), and on the seventh rank, where they attack pawns and trap the king. Each rook on an open file is
worth about 1.5 percentage points here.

## King safety and the pawn shield
A castled king is usually protected by the pawns in front of it, called the pawn shield. Each pawn in the shield adds
about 0.8 percentage points in this project, and each open file next to the king costs about 0.8. A king left in the
centre after move 20 also tends to be a liability.

## Opposite-side castling
When the two players castle on opposite sides, each can push pawns at the other king without weakening their own. Such
games are sharper: they are drawn less often (3.8% against 4.7% when both castle on the same side).

## Space and the centre
Space is how many squares a player controls in the opponent's half of the board. More space gives pieces more room.
Controlling the four centre squares (d4, e4, d5, e5) is a basic opening and middlegame goal.

## Development
Development means moving pieces off their starting squares into play. Being ahead in development, especially of
knights and bishops, is an advantage: each extra developed minor piece is worth about 1.6 percentage points here.

## Trading queens
When both queens come off the board, games become calmer and draws become much more likely: 6.8% to 8.4% of games with
a queen trade are drawn, against 1.7% when the queens stay on.
